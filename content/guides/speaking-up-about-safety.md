Speaking up is part of safe practice. It is also awkward, especially when you are new and the person you need to question has been there for years. This page is an overview of the paths that exist in Australia. It is not legal advice, not clinical advice, and not a script for a particular emergency. If someone is in immediate danger, call 000, and use the internal emergency number as well.

OnlyScrubs is not AHPRA, not the NMBA, not a union and not your employer. We cannot lodge a notification for you, and we will not publish the story of what happened.

## Start with the safety problem in front of you

Most concerns are fixed, or at least raised, inside the shift. The person in charge, your preceptor, or your facilitator is the first call for "I am not safe to do this alone", "this workload means breaks are not happening", or "I think this situation needs a review." Be specific. What you saw, in professional language. What you need them to do. What you will do if they are not available.

You do not need a perfect speech. You need a sentence you can repeat later: "I am escalating this because I am worried about safety." Write the time down in a private note afterwards. Do not write a patient's name, a bed number, or a diagnosis in a notebook you take home, and do not put that note on the internet. The [asking for help](/guides/asking-for-help.html) guide is the everyday version of this conversation.

Students raise placement concerns with the university as well as the site. The university holds the agreement. New graduates raise program concerns with the educator. If you are employed, your service will have an incident process. Use the one they name. This website is not that process.

## Workplace reporting is not the same as a notification

An incident report, a risk report, or a speaking-up program inside a health service is how that service reviews what happened. It stays in their system. A notification to Ahpra is a different legal step about a registered health practitioner's conduct, health or performance. You might do one, or both, or neither, depending on the facts. Do not assume that telling your manager has notified the Board. Do not assume that a Board notification replaces the local incident process.

Fatigue, short staffing and broken equipment are usually workplace safety issues. Safe Work Australia's material on [fatigue](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/safety-topic/hazards/fatigue) is the general hazard framing. Your state work health and safety regulator is the local one. A union can explain the industrial path if you are a member. None of those is a clinical instruction.

## Mandatory notifications, in overview

Some practitioners must notify Ahpra about certain risks. The overview is on Ahpra's [mandatory notifications](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Notifications/mandatorynotifications.aspx) page, and the NMBA points to the [guidelines for mandatory notifications](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Statements/Codes-Guidelines/Guidelines-for-mandatory-notifications.aspx). Read those before you decide that a situation is, or is not, notifiable. This summary is only a map.

For registered practitioners, the guidelines describe notifiable conduct in four broad areas: practising while intoxicated by alcohol or drugs, sexual misconduct in connection with practice, an impairment that places the public at substantial risk of harm, and a significant departure from professional standards that places the public at risk of harm. The words "substantial" and "significant" are doing legal work. A mistake, a single sharp comment, or a personality clash is not automatically any of those things. The guideline, not a group chat, is where the threshold is explained.

Students are treated differently. The obligation to notify about a student is narrower and is centred on impairment that places the public at substantial risk. Again, the guideline has the detail. Do not rely on this paragraph if you are the person who has to decide.

Anyone can make a voluntary notification. Mandatory means some people must notify in some situations, not that every worry must be sent to Ahpra. If you are unsure, you can ask Ahpra, your insurer, your educator or your union without posting the facts publicly. Do not include the clinical story in an email to OnlyScrubs. We cannot action it, and the inbox is the wrong place for it.

## How to say it without harming the process

Stick to what you observed and what you did. Avoid insults, diagnoses of your colleagues, and motive guessing. "I am concerned about safety" ages better than "they are incompetent." Do not discuss the matter on social media, even in a "private" story. The [social media guide](/guides/social-media-and-professionalism.html) is the reason. A post can identify a service and can become evidence you did not intend to create.

If you are worried about retaliation, say that to the person who receives the report and ask what protection the process includes. Document the dates of your reports. Keep that record private.

The [NMBA standards for practice](/guides/nmba-standards-for-practice.html) expect you to work within your capability and to practise safely. Speaking up is part of that. It is not disloyalty. A culture that punishes the question is a culture to tell your educator or your union about, in the proper channel.

## After you have spoken

You may not see the outcome. Confidential processes often cannot tell you what happened next. That silence is frustrating and it is not proof that nothing occurred. Use your support people. Nurse & Midwife Support is [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/). If the situation has left you frightened or unable to sleep, your GP and the lines on the [wellbeing page](/wellbeing.html) are the right next call. Call 000 if there is immediate danger.

You did the professional thing when you raised a safety concern in the right room. The internet is not that room.
