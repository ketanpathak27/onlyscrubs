The graduate year is a job with a learning structure wrapped around it. The structure is not the same in every state, and it is not the same in public and private employers. Anyone who describes "the" grad program as if it were one national course is guessing.

This is general information about starting work. It is not employment advice, industrial advice, or clinical instruction.

## Recruitment is a calendar, not a vibe

Public health services usually run their own graduate recruitment rounds, with their own opening and closing dates. Private hospitals and aged care employers often recruit on a different timetable, sometimes later, sometimes directly. Put the dates in a calendar as soon as you see them. Missing a round is common and painful. It is not a judgement on whether you can do the work.

Read what the round actually asks for: preferred areas, referee details, registration timing, and whether you must already hold registration with the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia or whether a pending application is accepted. Those rules change. The [NMBA site](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/) is the place for registration facts, not a Facebook comment. The steps for a new graduate are on [AHPRA registration as a new grad](/guides/ahpra-registration-new-grad.html), and the state rounds are on [graduate programs by state](/guides/graduate-programs-by-state.html).

Have a referee who has seen you in practice and who has agreed to be contacted. Ask them before you write their name. Give them the dates so they are not surprised by an email on a day off.

## The first weeks on the floor

Many programs include a period where you are extra to the numbers, often called supernumerary time. How long it lasts, and what you are expected to carry, varies. Ask in the first week: who is your educator, what "supernumerary" means on this roster, and which things you should not do alone yet.

You are not employed as someone who already knows the local drugs cupboard, the local software, or the local after-hours process. You are employed as a new registrant who will learn them. Asking on day two is safer than improvising on day twelve.

Learn the escalation path as a fact, the way you learn the door code. Who do you call first? Who if they do not answer? What is the emergency number inside the building, as well as 000? Write it where you can see it, without patient information beside it.

## The feeling of being a fraud

Most new grads have a week where they are sure the offer was a clerical error. That feeling is common. It is not evidence. Evidence is the feedback you ask for.

Ask your preceptor for one specific thing after a shift, not "was I okay?". Use the same shape as a good handover of your own learning: what you did, what you were unsure about, what you want to try next time. The [ISBAR guide](/guides/isbar-handover.html) is about talking to colleagues. The habit of being specific helps here too.

If the feeling comes with not sleeping, panic before every shift, or dread that does not ease after the first month, treat that as something to act on. Talk to the educator, your GP, or Nurse & Midwife Support on [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/). You do not have to wait until you are unsafe.

## Documentation and scope now that you are registered

Registration changes the responsibility. You are accountable for what you sign. If you did not do it, or you did not see it, do not let a busy shift talk you into clicking as if you did. Policies exist for verbal orders, alterations, and late entries. Use them. Do not invent a workaround because the computer is slow.

You will be asked to do things you have not done since a lab, or have never done. The sentence is still: "I have not done this independently. I need someone with me the first time." A good team expects that sentence. A team that mocks it is a team to tell your educator about. The [asking for help guide](/guides/asking-for-help.html) is that sentence, and what to do after a hard shift, without turning it into a public story.

## Rosters, nights, and leaving on time

Graduate rosters often include nights and weekends early. Read the [night shift guide](/guides/night-shift.html) before the first one, and plan the sleep and the drive while you are still on a day off. Leaving late every day is not a badge. If handover is routinely unfinished because the load is unreasonable, that is a conversation with your educator or your union, not a personal failing you fix by skipping breaks forever. This page is not industrial advice. Your union or employer agreement is.

## If the program is a poor fit

People move areas, pause, or leave grad programs. That happens for family reasons, health, bullying, or a specialty that was wrong on paper. Talk to the educator before you disappear. There is often a process, and there is often another start date. Quitting by silence makes the next application harder to explain.

Keep a short, private record of your own learning goals and the feedback you received, with no patient details and no gossip. It helps at performance reviews, and it helps on the days you can only remember the mistake.

You are allowed to be new. The job is to become safe and steady, not to look finished.
