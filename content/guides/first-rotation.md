The first rotation feels like everyone else has a map you were not given. They do. They have worked there longer. Your job in the first weeks is to learn the map, not to look as if you wrote it. This is general information for students and new graduates in Australia. It is not clinical advice, and it will not tell you how to perform a procedure.

If you want the longer picture of a graduate year, read [your first graduate year](/guides/first-graduate-year.html). If you are still on placement, [your first clinical placement](/guides/first-clinical-placement.html) sits beside this page. Handover language is in the [ISBAR guide](/guides/isbar-handover.html).

## Learn the people before the cupboard

On day one, write down who is who, in a notebook that stays in your pocket and contains no patient information. Who is the person in charge of the shift? Who is your preceptor, buddy or facilitator? Who is the educator, and how do you actually reach them? What is the internal emergency number, as well as 000?

Names of colleagues belong in your private notebook, not in a message to a friend and not on this website. "My preceptor" is enough in public. If the relationship is already difficult, there is a separate page on [dealing with a difficult preceptor](/guides/difficult-preceptor.html) that stays at that same level of detail.

Ask, in the first week, what you are not expected to do alone yet. The sentence is specific: "I have not done this independently here. I need someone with me the first time." A good team expects it. The [NMBA standards](/guides/nmba-standards-for-practice.html) describe capability in that spirit. They do not list your local tasks. Your educator does.

## Learn the rhythm of the shift

Every unit has a shape to the day: when handover happens, when rounds happen, when breaks are supposed to happen, when the pharmacy or the kitchen stops taking requests. You will not know it on Monday. By Friday you should be able to say it back. Ask a colleague to walk you through "a normal morning here" without using a real person's story as the example.

Write the local names of forms and software. The same task has different screens in different services. Nobody is testing whether you memorised a brand. They are testing whether you can find the thing you are supposed to sign.

Leaving late every day is not proof you are conscientious. If handover is unfinished because the load is unreasonable, that is a conversation with your educator, not a personality flaw you fix by skipping food. Breaks are part of being safe for the next hour. Fatigue is covered in [managing fatigue](/guides/fatigue-and-night-shift-sleep.html) and in the [night shift guide](/guides/night-shift.html) if the rotation includes nights.

## What to carry, and what to leave out of your bag

A pen you like, a small notebook, and a way to drink water will do more than a new gadget. The gear notes on this site stay general. Do not buy a pile of equipment because a video said new grads need it.

Do not carry patient lists home. Do not photograph a screen "to study later." Do not put a handover sheet in a car door. Those are privacy failures, not study techniques. If you want a record of your own learning, write "I practised a skill with supervision" and the date of the shift, with no identifiers. The [social media guide](/guides/social-media-and-professionalism.html) is the same rule for your phone.

## Questions that are worth asking every week

Pick one question a week so you do not arrive with twenty and then ask none.

- What should I be doing without being asked, and what must I check first?
- Who do I call if the person in charge is off the floor?
- What does a good handover sound like here?
- Which policy covers the thing I keep improvising?
- What should I read before the next study day?

Write the answer in your own words. If the answer is "just watch," ask what you are watching for. Watching without a point becomes hiding.

## The feeling that you are behind

Most people in a first rotation have a week where they are sure the offer was a mistake. That feeling is common. It is not evidence. Evidence is the feedback you ask for: one specific thing you did, one thing you were unsure about, one thing you will try next time. "Was I okay?" is a hard question to answer. "Was my handover in the order you wanted?" is not.

If the feeling comes with dread that does not ease, or you are not sleeping, talk to your educator, your GP, or Nurse & Midwife Support on [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/). You do not have to wait until you are unsafe. Support lines are listed on the [wellbeing page](/wellbeing.html).

You are allowed to be new for the whole rotation. The aim is to be safe and a little more steady at the end of it, not to look finished.
