Your first placement is a change of pace, not a test of whether you were born for the work. Universities describe it in learning outcomes. The shift itself is louder, earlier, and more full of names than the handbook suggests. You can prepare the boring parts so the new parts have room.

This is general information for students in Australia. It is not clinical instruction, and it does not replace your university facilitator, the placement brief, or the rules of the service you are visiting.

## Before the roster starts

Read the placement letter twice. Note the start time, the meeting point, the uniform colour, and who to call if you are sick or late. Those details differ between universities and between public and private services. A group chat rumour is a poor source.

Sort transport for the earliest shift, not the average one. If you are driving, look at parking rules and cost. If you are on public transport, check the first service of the day, then a backup. Tell someone at home what time you expect to be back, without describing the workplace.

Pack from the list your university gave you. Typical items include an approved uniform, closed shoes, your student ID, and any compliance documents they told you to carry or to have uploaded. The tick list on [Student Bay](/student-bay.html) is the same idea in a form you can print. Do not invent extra clinical kit to look prepared. If the brief says the service supplies a particular item, believe the brief. What it costs to get there is covered, without invented prices, in the [budgeting guide](/guides/budgeting.html).

Wash a new uniform before day one. Fabric shrinks, and a too-small set is a distraction you do not need while you are trying to remember where the staff room is.

## The first morning

Arrive early enough to find the entrance, the change space, and the person who is expecting you. Introduce yourself with your name, your year, and the fact that you are a student. Ask what they would like you to call them. Ask, early, what you are allowed to do today and what must wait for a registered nurse or midwife.

You are not behind because you do not know the store-room code. You are behind if you pretend you do. A useful sentence is: "I have not done this here before. Can I watch once, then try with you beside me?" That is professional, not weak.

Write down the names of your buddy and your facilitator, and how to reach them during the shift. Keep that note free of anything that identifies a person in your care.

## Scope, notes, and phones

Your scope is set by your course, your year level, and the policies of the placement. If you are unsure whether you can do something, you do not do it until a registered person has said you can, in the way your university expects. "Everyone else was busy" is not a scope.

Notes are where students get into trouble without meaning to. Follow the documentation rules you were taught. Do not copy identifiers into a personal notebook that leaves the building. Do not photograph a screen, a wristband, a whiteboard, or a corridor. Do not debrief the day on social media, even if you think you have removed the names. Small details still identify people in a small community.

Phones stay where the local rule says they stay. If you need your phone for a timetable or a train, ask when you may look at it.

## Learning on a busy shift

Pick one or two skills or routines to understand properly, rather than collecting a blur of tasks. At a quiet moment, ask your buddy what "a good student afternoon" looks like to them. People are kinder when they can see you are trying to fit their ward's rhythm, not to perform a private checklist.

If you are corrected, write the correction down in general terms and thank them. Arguing about technique in front of a patient, or in a corridor, rarely helps. If the correction itself feels unsafe or humiliating, that is a facilitator conversation, not a silent endurance test. See [how to ask for help](/guides/asking-for-help.html).

Eat something. Students skip breaks to look committed, then think badly at 2pm. Ask when the usual break is, and take it unless there is a clear reason not to. Hydrate. Placement courage is not dehydration.

## Feedback without the spiral

Ask for feedback while the shift is still recent: one thing to keep, and one thing to change. "How did I go?" is too wide, and busy people answer "fine". Specific is easier: "Was my introduction clear?" or "Did I get in the way during that admission?"

You will have a shift that feels clumsy. That is information about a new environment, not a verdict on your registration. If several shifts feel unsafe, or you are being asked to work outside your scope, contact your university facilitator the same day. Waiting until the written assessment is how small problems become failed placements.

## A simple reset for the drive home

Before you leave, check you have your own pen, your ID, and no paperwork that is not yours. If you are tired enough that driving worries you, say so before you are in the car. Fatigue on the road is a safety issue, including on early finishes after a bad night's sleep. The [night shift guide](/guides/night-shift.html) and the planner on the [Shift Kit](/shift-kit.html) page are general tools, not personal advice.

Nurse & Midwife Support is there for students as well as registered staff: [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/). You do not have to be in crisis to call.
