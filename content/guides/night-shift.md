Night shift asks your body to be alert while everyone else is asleep, then to sleep while the day is happening outside the blinds. You will not hack that perfectly. You can make it less brutal, and you can refuse to drive when you are not safe.

This is general sleep-hygiene information, not a medical plan. If you are pregnant, have a sleep disorder, take medicines that affect alertness, or you have been told to limit caffeine, follow that advice instead of a webpage.

## Decide the sleep before you start the run

A common pattern is a main sleep after you get home, plus a shorter nap before the next night if you need one. The [shift planner](/shift-kit.html) on this site does that arithmetic on your phone and does not upload it. Use it as a sketch. Your roster, your commute, and the people you live with will edit it.

For a night that finishes in the early morning, plan to be in bed within about an hour and a half of finishing, after food and a shower if you want one. Protect a block of several hours in a dark, cool room. A ninety-minute nap in the late afternoon, ending with enough time to wake up, eat, and travel, helps some people face the next night. It makes other people feel worse. Try it on a day off before you rely on it.

Tell the people you live with when you are sleeping. A vacuum at 10am is not a moral failing on their part if you never said you were in bed. Earplugs and an eye mask are cheaper than another coffee.

## Caffeine is a tool with a cutoff

A widely used rule of thumb is to stop caffeine about six hours before you intend to sleep. On a night shift that can mean coffee or tea early in the shift, and none in the second half, if you plan to sleep in the morning. Energy drinks on top of coffee are how people miss the cutoff without noticing.

If caffeine makes you anxious, shaky, or unable to sleep even when you are exhausted, use less, earlier. The goal is a functional night, not a personality made of sugar and guarana.

The [Sleep Health Foundation](https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/) publishes general Australian information on sleep. It is a better place for sleep facts than a group chat.

## Food, light, and the 3am slump

Eat a proper meal before the shift if you can. Pack food you can eat cold or quickly. The canteen, if there is one, may be shut. A sugary snack at 3am feels like help and then feels like a slump.

Bright light at the start of a night can help you feel more awake. Dimmer light on the way home, and sunglasses if the morning sun hits you in the car, helps some people avoid feeling suddenly wired at 7am. Do not drive in sunglasses so dark you cannot see. This is a comfort trick, not a treatment.

Alcohol does not improve daytime sleep. It shortens it and makes it lighter. If the shift was hard, alcohol is also a poor debrief. See [how to ask for help](/guides/asking-for-help.html).

## The drive home is part of the shift

Drowsy driving kills people, including healthcare workers finishing nights. If you are nodding, drifting in the lane, or you cannot remember the last few minutes of road, you are not "a bit tired". You stop. Say so to the person in charge before you leave if you are still at work. Use whatever fatigue process your workplace has. A nap in a quiet room, a colleague who is actually awake, or a train are all better than hoping.

Plan the trip while you are still sharp: the route, the time it takes, and what you will do if you feel unsafe. Students should tell their facilitator if they are being rostered in a way that makes the commute dangerous. You are allowed to raise that.

## Days off are not a moral test

After a run of nights, one long sleep will not reset you. A short second sleep, daylight, and a normal meal time help more than staying in a dark room until the evening out of guilt. If you have another night coming, do not flip fully back to daytime hours and then expect the next night to feel easy.

Social plans at 11am on your sleep day can wait. Friends who have never worked nights may need one clear sentence: "I am asleep then. I can do tomorrow afternoon."

## When nights are costing more than tiredness

Low mood, using alcohol or other drugs to get to sleep, or dreading every roster are reasons to talk to someone, not reasons to be tougher. Your GP is the right person for health concerns. For nurses, midwives and students, Nurse & Midwife Support is on [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/), 24 hours. If you are in immediate danger, call 000.

Nothing on this page tells you how to manage a patient overnight. Your workplace education and your own registration or student scope do that. A longer note on fatigue, driving and who to tell is in [managing fatigue and night-shift sleep](/guides/fatigue-and-night-shift-sleep.html).
