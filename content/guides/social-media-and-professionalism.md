The group chat feels private right up until it is not. A story, a photo of a screen, a joke that names a ward: any of those can identify a person or a service, and any of them can sit on a server you do not control. This page is general information about professionalism online. It is not clinical advice, and it is not a ruling. The documents that bind you are the NMBA's and Ahpra's.

OnlyScrubs is not affiliated with either of them. We follow their public guidance because it is the right boundary for a site like this, not because we speak for them.

## The guidance to actually open

Read the NMBA's [social media guidance](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Statements/Codes-Guidelines/Social-media-guidance.aspx). It explains how the codes and the National Law show up when you post, comment, or message. Then read Ahpra's [advertising hub](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub.aspx). Advertising rules apply when you promote a regulated health service, including your own side work. They are not only for clinics with a billboard.

The [code of conduct](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Statements/Professional-standards.aspx) sits underneath both. Respect, privacy, and professional boundaries do not pause because you are in your own clothes and the shift has finished.

## What not to post, even if you think it is de-identified

A patient story is still a patient story when you remove the name. Small details add up: a rare situation, a shift date, a photo of a doorway, a comment that only one team would understand. The NMBA guidance is blunt about confidentiality. If you would not say it in the hospital cafeteria with a stranger at the next table, do not put it online.

The same rule is why OnlyScrubs will not publish confessions that name a hospital, a colleague, a bed, or a clinical incident. The [submission rules](/submission-rules.html) are the local version of that guidance. "But it is funny" is not an exception.

Do not photograph a whiteboard, a medication chart, a wristband, or a screen. Do not post a handover sheet to ask a friend for help. Ask the person who is actually supervising you. The [asking for help](/guides/asking-for-help.html) guide is about that conversation.

## Jokes, the Q word, and the line

You can be funny about the job's absurdity. Rosters, cold coffee, the printer, the full moon, paperwork, short staffing as a system problem: those are the tearoom. The professions' public trust is not a punchline. Do not mock a patient's condition, body, disability, death, or family. Do not post a photo of someone in your care, even if they "would not mind" and even if their face is cropped. Consent for a selfie is not something you collect in a corridor.

Do not name a colleague to shame them. A difficult preceptor is a supervision problem to raise with your educator or university, not a thread. See [dealing with a difficult preceptor](/guides/difficult-preceptor.html).

Swearing in a private kitchen is your business. A public slur, a threat, or a pile-on is a professional problem as well as a moderation problem. This site holds that kind of post for a person to review. It does not celebrate it.

## Advertising, testimonials, and side hustles

If you sell a service that is a regulated health service, Ahpra's advertising rules apply. Testimonials about clinical care are not allowed. Neither are claims you cannot back, or phrases that pressure someone into unnecessary care. "My patients say I am the best" is a testimonial even when you are proud of the work.

Templates, study notes and planners are not a health service. They still should not pretend to be official, approved, or endorsed by the NMBA or Ahpra. The OnlyScrubs shop says that in plain words on purpose. If you ever sell your own notes, use the same honesty. "I wrote this to organise my study" is a true sentence. "NMBA approved" is not, unless the Board has actually said so, which it has not said about a random PDF.

## Students

Students are not yet on the register in the same way, and the expectations still apply. Universities and placement agreements usually say so. A post that identifies a placement site can end the placement. It can also follow you into a registration application if it shows a serious disregard for privacy. The [standards guide](/guides/nmba-standards-for-practice.html) is worth reading before you need it in a meeting.

Separate accounts do not separate you from your profession. Patients, colleagues and employers find the "private" one. If you would be unhappy to read the post out at a job interview, delete it. If it is already shared, assume you cannot get every copy back, and talk to your educator early rather than hoping.

## A pocket rule

Before you post, ask three questions. Does this identify a person or a service? Does this claim a clinical result or invite a testimonial? Would I be comfortable if my facilitator, my employer and the Board all read it with their name on the badge? If any answer worries you, do not post it. Tell the joke in the tearoom, about the roster, and leave the rest off the internet.
