Feet and backs take the hours that rosters do not count. This page is about habits and buying sense. It is not physiotherapy, not a diagnosis, and not permission to ignore a workplace manual-handling policy.

If you have pain that is new, severe, one-sided, or paired with numbness, chest symptoms, or bladder or bowel changes, stop reading and get urgent clinical care. Call 000 if you are in immediate danger. For everything else that worries you, your GP or a physiotherapist who has actually assessed you is the right next step.

## Shoes do more than match the uniform

Wear the shoe your workplace allows, then choose the most comfortable one inside that rule. Closed toe, closed heel, and a sole that still has tread are the usual baseline. The [gear notes on shoes](/gear-guide.html) cover what to look for in a shop without pretending there is one correct brand.

Replace shoes when they lean to one side or the cushioning has gone flat. Two pairs, rotated, dry out between shifts and often last longer than one pair worn every day. Socks matter. A damp cotton sock in a hot shoe is a blister factory. Change them if you can at a break.

Compression socks are popular on long standing shifts. They are not a treatment you prescribe for yourself from a website. The [sock notes](/gear-guide.html) explain the cautions, including who should ask their own clinician first.

## Standing, when the work allows it

Shift your weight. Use a footrest or a low shelf if one is provided and it is not a trip hazard. Sit for tasks that can be done sitting: some notes, some phone calls, some feeds, depending on the setting and the policy. Heroic standing is not a clinical outcome.

If you are in theatre or a bay where you cannot sit, a planned change of position and shoes that fit are the realistic tools. Tell someone if you feel faint. That is a safety issue, not an inconvenience.

## Loads and the awkward lift

Manual handling training exists because backs are injured by ordinary, rushed lifts, not only by dramatic ones. Use the equipment your workplace provides: slides, hoists, and extra hands. If the equipment is missing or broken, say so and follow the incident process. Do not "just do it this once" as the culture.

General principles you will have been taught, and should still follow rather than replace with this page: get help early, move the person or object close to you, avoid twisting while you hold a load, and do not lift what the policy says to hoist. If your training and this paragraph ever seem to disagree, follow your training.

Students should not be the extra pair of hands that makes an unsafe lift look staffed. You can say, "I have not been shown this hoist. I can go and get someone who has."

## Backs that ache after every shift

Muscle ache after a new run of shifts can settle with rest, a change of shoes, and not adding a gym personal best on the same day. Pain that wakes you, travels down a leg, or keeps you off the next shift needs a proper assessment. Do not ask a group chat to name the structure you have hurt.

Heat, stretches you were given by someone who assessed you, and pacing are individual. Copying a video because a colleague liked it is how people aggravate things. A physiotherapist or GP can give you a plan that matches your job.

Report work injuries the way your employer requires, and do it early. Late reports are harder for everyone. This is not legal advice. Your employer’s process and, if you need it, your union are the places for that.

## Small habits that add up

- Tie shoes properly. A loose heel slips and your toes grip all day.
- Keep nails trimmed so they are not stabbing the toe box by hour eight.
- Put your bag on both shoulders. A one-shoulder tote full of textbooks is its own injury. See [bags](/gear-guide.html).
- On nights, fatigue makes lifting sloppier. The [night shift guide](/guides/night-shift.html) is about alertness, which is part of looking after your back.
- Drink water. It will not fix a disc. It does stop some of the headaches people blame on their shoes.

Look after the body that has to come back tomorrow. That is allowed, including on a busy ward, and including when you are a student trying to look keen.
