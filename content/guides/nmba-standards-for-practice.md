The Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia writes the professional standards that apply to nurses and midwives. Universities, employers and the public register all point back to them. This page is a reading guide. It is not clinical advice, and it is not the standards. If you need the wording that matters in a reflection, an interview or a notification, open the [professional standards](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Statements/Professional-standards.aspx) on the NMBA site and quote from there.

## There is more than one document

People say "the standards" as if there were a single page. There are several, and they do different jobs.

The [registered nurse standards for practice](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Statements/Professional-standards/registered-nurse-standards-for-practice.aspx) describe what safe registered-nurse practice looks like. The [midwife standards for practice](https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes-Guidelines-Statements/Professional-standards/midwife-standards-for-practice.aspx) do that job for midwifery. Enrolled nurses have their own standards. A code of conduct sets out the behaviour the public can expect. There is also a code of ethics the Board points to. Registration standards, such as CPD, criminal history, English language and professional indemnity, are a different pile again. They are about staying registered, not about how to do a task on a shift.

Use the document that matches the question. "How should I behave toward a colleague?" is not answered by the fee schedule. "Can I renew?" is not answered by a standard for practice.

## The registered nurse standards, in ordinary words

The registered nurse standards are built as seven statements. This is a paraphrase so you can find your place. The official text is shorter and more precise than a study summary, and it is the one that counts.

1. **Thinks critically and analyses nursing practice.** You do not copy a routine because it is the routine. You can say what you are basing a decision on, including the person's own goals, and you notice when the situation has changed.
2. **Engages in therapeutic and professional relationships.** Care is a relationship with the person, and a working relationship with colleagues. It includes respect, privacy, and knowing the boundary of your role.
3. **Maintains the capability for practice.** You keep yourself able to do the work: learning, reflection, and knowing when you are not capable, including when you are too tired or too new. [CPD for new grads](/guides/cpd-for-new-grads.html) sits under this idea. So does saying "I have not done this independently."
4. **Comprehensively conducts assessments.** Assessment is systematic and includes what the person says, not only a form. This page will not teach you an assessment. Your program and your workplace do that.
5. **Develops a plan for nursing practice.** A plan is agreed, documented in the way the service requires, and changed when the assessment changes. It is not a private guess you keep in your pocket.
6. **Provides safe, appropriate and responsive quality nursing practice.** The work itself is matched to the plan, to the person's needs, and to your scope. Delegation and supervision live here. You do not hand a task to someone who cannot do it, and you do not take a task you cannot do.
7. **Evaluates outcomes.** You look at whether the plan did what it was for, and you use that to change the next decision. Evaluation is part of the work, not an essay you add at the end of a placement.

Midwifery standards use midwifery language and a midwifery scope. Do not force the nursing list onto midwifery practice. Open the midwife document.

## How students and new grads actually use them

On placement, facilitators often ask you to link a reflection to a standard. Pick the standard the moment actually shows. "I asked for help before I gave a medicine I had not given alone" is about capability and safe practice. It is not all seven. Name the one or two that fit, in your own words, and say what you will do next time. A paragraph that quotes every standard and describes no behaviour is easy to spot.

In a graduate interview, the same habit helps. A question about a difficult day is often a question about standards: who you told, what you did not do, how you protected privacy. You can answer without naming a hospital, a patient or a date. If you cannot tell the story without those details, it is the wrong story for an interview and the wrong story for the internet. The [submission rules](/submission-rules.html) on this site are the same boundary.

The standards are also why "everyone does it" is a weak sentence. A local shortcut that conflicts with the standard is a problem to raise, not a tradition to inherit. [Speaking up](/guides/speaking-up-about-safety.html) is the overview of that step. It is not legal advice.

## What the standards are not

They are not a textbook of tasks. They will not tell you a dose, a dressing, or what to say in a particular clinical emergency. They are not a performance score your employer must copy word for word, though many appraisals are built from them. They are not optional on a bad shift.

They also do not belong to OnlyScrubs. We can point at them. We cannot interpret them for your case. If a notification, a university hearing or an employer process is underway, read the document and get advice from someone who can act for you, such as your education provider, your insurer or your union. This site is none of those.

Read them once while you are still a student, slowly, with a cup of tea. Then keep the link. The day you need a sentence, you will want the Board's sentence, not a memory of this page.
