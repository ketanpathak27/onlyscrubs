Nursing and midwifery degrees in Australia are heavy on both theory and placement. The students who cope are not always the ones who highlight the most pages. They are usually the ones who practise remembering, and who protect sleep during assessment weeks.

These are study habits, not a promise about grades, and not clinical advice.

## Start from the unit outline

The outline tells you what can be examined and what the placement is assessing. Read the learning outcomes before you read the textbook chapter. If a topic is not in the outcomes, it can still be interesting. It should not eat the night before a quiz that is about something else.

Past papers and practice quizzes, when your university provides them, are worth more than rereading. Rereading feels productive because the page is familiar. Familiar is not the same as retrievable at 9am in an exam hall.

## Active recall, in plain language

Close the notes and write what you remember. Say it aloud as if you were explaining it to a classmate who missed the lecture. Check what you missed, then do it again tomorrow, not five more times tonight.

A simple loop:

1. Read a small section with a question in mind.
2. Shut it.
3. Answer the question from memory, in your own words.
4. Check, and mark the gap.
5. Come back to the gap the next day.

Teach-back works for skills labs too. After a lab, write the steps in order without looking, then check the lab sheet. Do not turn that list into a set of instructions you use on placement instead of the local policy. Lab steps and workplace steps are not always the same.

## Placement weeks are a different semester

Trying to keep a full study timetable during a full-time placement is how people revise badly at midnight. During placement, aim for short sessions: twenty to forty minutes on the topic that placement is making real, then stop. Use the commute only if you are a passenger. Do not quiz yourself while you drive.

Carry questions, not patient details. "I want to understand the difference between these two terms we used in class" is a study note. A name, a bed, a date, and a story is not.

When you are back in theory blocks, rebuild the longer sessions. Spaced practice beats a single Sunday panic. If you only have one Sunday, you still start with practice questions, not with colour-coding the entire reader.

## Groups, and the group chat

A small study group helps if you set a question and a finish time. It hurts if it becomes a competition about who is most anxious. Leave chats that share copyrighted lecture slides against the university's rules, or that swap placement stories with identifiers. Both can become academic or professional problems.

If you are working casual shifts as well as studying, put the shifts in the same calendar as assessments. A late shift the night before an exam is a choice you should see coming, not a surprise on the roster app.

## Sleep is a study tool

You encode less when you are awake all night with caffeine. For exam weeks, keep a regular sleep time if you can, and stop caffeine early enough that you can actually fall asleep. The six-hour rule of thumb is explained in the [night shift guide](/guides/night-shift.html). It is a rule of thumb, not a prescription.

If concentration has fallen apart for weeks, or you are using alcohol or other substances to get through assessments, talk to your university counselling service or your GP. That is health care, not a study hack. Students can also call Nurse & Midwife Support on [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/).

## What not to buy

You do not need a new app for every unit, a wall of sticky notes, or a more expensive stethoscope to pass pathophysiology. You need the readings that are actually set, a way to test your memory, and enough sleep to use both. The [gear guide](/gear-guide.html) is about shift kit, not about stationery that promises a HD.

If money is the thing making study harder, the [budgeting guide](/guides/budgeting.html) covers the costs that show up in Australian courses, including unpaid placement. When the library weeks turn into shifts, use the [first placement guide](/guides/first-clinical-placement.html). It is about showing up, not about another textbook.
