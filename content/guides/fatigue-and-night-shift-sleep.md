Night shift asks you to be awake while the suburb is asleep, and then to sleep while the bins are collected. Fatigue is what accumulates when that bargain is unfair, the roster is tight, or you are new and running on nerves. This page is general information. It is not clinical advice, not a diagnosis, and not a reason to take a medicine because a stranger online takes one.

The shorter, practical version of the sleep sketch is the [night shift guide](/guides/night-shift.html). The calculator on the [shift kit](/shift-kit.html) stays on your phone. Nothing from it is uploaded. Use both as a sketch. Your body, your commute and any advice you have already been given outrank a webpage.

## Fatigue is a safety issue, not a personality

Being tired after a run of shifts is ordinary. Being so tired that you miss steps, snap at people, or cannot remember the drive home is a safety problem. Safe Work Australia treats [fatigue](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/safety-topic/hazards/fatigue) as a workplace hazard, not a private failing. Short staffing and odd rosters are system problems. You can still make the next hour safer while those problems exist.

Tell the person in charge if you are not safe to keep going or not safe to drive. That sentence is allowed. It is not a confession of weakness. Students should also tell their facilitator if the roster makes the commute dangerous. New graduates should tell their educator. The [first rotation](/guides/first-rotation.html) page is about those early questions.

If you are drowsy, do not drive. That line is not a slogan. A nap in a quiet room, a colleague who can drop you at a station, or a delay until you are alert will beat a brave trip on a freeway. Your workplace should have a fatigue process. Ask where it is written before you need it at 7am.

## Sleep, in general terms

Healthdirect's pages on [sleep](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/sleep) are the public, non-commercial explanation of ordinary sleep. They are a better source than a supplement ad. A common pattern after a night shift is a main sleep when you get home, in a dark, cool, quiet room, and sometimes a shorter nap before the next night. Some people feel worse after the nap. Try the pattern on a day off before you depend on it.

A rule of thumb used in sleep hygiene is to leave caffeine alone in the hours before you plan to sleep. The shift kit uses about six hours as that sketch. People differ. If a clinician has told you to limit caffeine, follow that advice rather than a calculator.

Light matters. Bright light on the way home can tell your brain that the day has started, which is the opposite of what you want if you are about to sleep. Sunglasses on the commute home help some people. A dark bedroom helps more than another scroll through a bright phone. This is general sleep hygiene, not a treatment plan.

Alcohol is a poor sleep strategy. It can make you drowsy and then fragment the sleep. It is also a bad idea before a drive. If you are using alcohol or other drugs to get to sleep after every night, that is a reason to talk to your GP, not a roster hack.

## Food, breaks, and the cold coffee

Eat something you would recognise as food. A shift that runs on leftover biscuits is a funny confession and a poor plan. Breaks are part of staying safe for the next part of the shift. Skipping them to look keen is how fatigue gets a head start. If breaks are routinely impossible because of the staffing, that is a workload conversation with the person in charge or your union, not a personal virtue.

Cold coffee is a tearoom joke. It is also a clue that the cup sat there while you did something else. Drink water as well. None of this replaces advice you have been given about diet, pregnancy, diabetes or medicines. If those apply to you, your own clinician is the person to ask. This page will not suggest doses, melatonin, or sleeping tablets.

## When tired becomes something else

Low mood, dread before every shift, or not sleeping even when you are in bed are reasons to get help. They are common in a hard roster and they are still worth acting on. Your GP can assess your health. Nurses, midwives and students can call Nurse & Midwife Support on [1800 667 877](https://www.nmsupport.org.au/), any time. Lifeline is 13 11 14. Beyond Blue is 1300 22 4636. Those numbers are repeated on the [wellbeing page](/wellbeing.html), which stays serious on purpose. If you are in immediate danger, call 000.

Do not post the details of how unwell you feel into a public confession if you want help. Posting is not a referral. The filter on this site will hold crisis language and show you the same support lines. A person still reads the queue. They are not a crisis service.

## The roster is allowed to be the problem

You can love the work and still say that six nights in a row is a design problem. Saying so to the right person, in a professional sentence, is not disloyal. [Speaking up about safety](/guides/speaking-up-about-safety.html) includes fatigue as a safety concern, in overview. It is not legal advice.

Protect the sleep you can protect. Tell the people you live with when you are in bed. Leave the group chat until the alarm. The shift will still be there. The bin truck might not wait, but the group chat can.
